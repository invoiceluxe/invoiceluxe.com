Most people know sales tax. Fewer know about **use tax**, which can quietly apply to business purchases and online orders. The two taxes work as a pair.

## The simple difference

- **Sales tax** is charged by the seller at the point of sale and sent to the state.
- **Use tax** is owed by the buyer when the seller did not charge sales tax on something the buyer uses, stores or consumes in their state.

Use tax exists so that buying from an out-of-state seller does not avoid tax. The rate is normally the same as the sales tax rate where you live.

## When do you owe use tax?

Common situations include:

- You buy equipment or supplies online and the seller does not charge tax.
- You buy items for your business tax-free using a resale certificate, then use them yourself instead of reselling them. See [resale certificates explained](/blog/resale-certificate-explained/).
- You buy something out of state and bring it home for use there.

## A worked example

You buy a $1,200 printer for your business from a seller who charges no tax. Your local combined rate is 7.25%.

Use tax owed: 1,200 × 0.0725 = **$87.00**

If you had paid 5% tax ($60.00) to another state on the same item, many states give a credit for that tax. In that case, you would owe about the difference: $87.00 − $60.00 = **$27.00**. Credit rules differ by state.

## How to report and pay use tax

- **Businesses with a sales tax permit** usually report use tax on their regular sales tax return.
- **Businesses without a permit** and **individuals** often use a separate use tax form or a line on the state income tax return.

Keep invoices for any purchase where no tax was charged, so you can calculate what you owe.

## Does use tax apply to freelancers?

It can. If you buy a computer, software or office furniture online without tax, you may owe use tax even if you do not charge sales tax to your clients. Whether services or digital items are taxable on your side depends on the state. See [are digital products and SaaS taxable](/blog/are-digital-products-and-saas-taxable/).

## Sales tax and use tax at a glance

| | Sales tax | Use tax |
|---|---|---|
| Who pays | Buyer, through the seller | Buyer, directly to the state |
| Who collects | Seller | Nobody; the buyer reports |
| When | At checkout | When no tax was charged |
| Rate | Local combined rate | Usually the same rate |

For the formula behind both, see [how to calculate sales tax](/blog/how-to-calculate-sales-tax/).

## Common situations

- Buying equipment or supplies from an out-of-state seller that did not charge tax
- Items you bought tax-free for resale and then used yourself, such as inventory you took home or gave away
- Software, subscriptions or services your state treats as taxable, when the vendor did not charge tax
- Items bought in a state with a lower rate and brought home for use

## Why states have use tax

Use tax closes a gap. Without it, buying from a seller that does not collect tax would be a way to avoid sales tax. States set the use tax rate to match the sales tax rate, so the buyer pays about the same either way.

## What you owe when you already paid some tax

If you paid sales tax to another state, many states give a credit for it. Suppose you bought an item and paid 4% elsewhere, and your home rate is 6%. You may owe the 2% difference. Check your state's credit rules.

## Keep a use tax record

A simple log makes filing easy:

| Date | Vendor | Item | Cost | Tax paid | Use tax due |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mar 4 | Online store | Monitor | $320.00 | $0.00 | $19.20 at 6% |
| Jun 12 | Supplier | Printer | $210.00 | $0.00 | $12.60 at 6% |

The amounts are examples. Sum the use tax due each period and report it on your sales and use tax return, or on your state income tax return if you are an individual.

## How states notice

Some states compare purchase records, vendor reports and audit results. A business may be asked about large equipment purchases. Keeping receipts and your log makes an audit simpler.

## For sellers

If you sell taxable items, you collect sales tax from your customers. Use tax is about what you buy for yourself. Keep both in mind when you prepare your tax filings. For more on when you must collect, see [what is sales tax nexus](/blog/what-is-sales-tax-nexus/), and for invoice layout, see [how to show sales tax on an invoice](/blog/how-to-show-sales-tax-on-an-invoice/).

## FAQ

### Do I owe use tax on online purchases?

Often yes, if the seller did not charge sales tax and the item is taxable in your state. Many sellers now collect tax, which solves the issue.

### Is use tax the same rate as sales tax?

Usually it matches your state and local sales tax rate.

### What happens if I never pay use tax?

States can audit and charge the tax plus interest and penalties. Use tax on business purchases is a common audit item.

### Do individuals owe use tax?

In many states, yes. You are expected to report use tax on taxable online purchases when the seller did not charge sales tax, though many individuals are not aware of it.

### Is use tax a separate tax I can avoid?

It is a legal obligation in states that have one. Check with your state tax agency or an accountant about how to report it.

### Is this tax advice?

No. It is a general overview. Ask your state revenue department or a tax professional about your situation.
