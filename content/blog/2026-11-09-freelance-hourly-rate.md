Many freelancers pick an hourly rate by copying someone else. A better way is to work backwards from the income you need and the hours you can realistically bill.

> **Hourly rate = (target income + business costs) ÷ billable hours**

## Step 1: set your income goal

Decide how much you want to earn in a year before tax. Example: **48,000**.

## Step 2: add your business costs

Add software, equipment, insurance, accountant fees, marketing and anything else you pay for work. Example: **6,000**.

Total you need to bill: 48,000 + 6,000 = **54,000**.

## Step 3: estimate your billable hours

You will not bill every hour you work. Admin, sales, emails and learning are unpaid.

| Item | Hours |
|---|---|
| 40 hours × 46 working weeks | 1,840 |
| Billable share (60%) | **1,104** |

## Step 4: divide

54,000 ÷ 1,104 = **48.91**, so charge about **49 per hour**.

If you can bill 75% of your time (1,380 hours), the rate falls to 54,000 ÷ 1,380 = 39.13. This shows why raising your billable share matters as much as raising your price.

## Remember tax and holidays

The calculation above is before income tax. Check what you will owe and decide whether to include it in your target. Holidays, sick days and quiet months are already reflected by using 46 weeks instead of 52.

## Should you charge hourly or per project?

Hourly rates are simple and fair when scope is unclear. Fixed prices suit clear projects and reward you for working faster. Many freelancers calculate an hourly rate first and then use it to price a project: estimated hours × rate. See [how to send a quote](/blog/how-to-send-a-quote-or-estimate/).

## Put your rate on the invoice

## FAQ

### How many billable hours does a freelancer have per year?

Often 1,000 to 1,500, depending on how much time goes to admin and finding work.

### Should I include VAT in my hourly rate?

State clearly whether your rate is before or after VAT. See [VAT inclusive vs exclusive](/blog/vat-inclusive-vs-exclusive/).

### How often should I raise my rate?

Review it at least once a year, and raise it for new clients first.

### Is this financial advice?

No. It is a simple method to estimate a rate. For tax and business decisions, speak to an accountant.
